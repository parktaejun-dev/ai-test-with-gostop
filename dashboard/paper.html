Risk-Sensitive LLM Evaluation in Four-Player Go-Stop: Remote-Model Panels, Scale Effects, and Policy Sensitivity

Go-Stop AI Evaluation Harness

Abstract: We evaluate fixed-policy large language model play in four-player Go-Stop, a Korean card game that compresses hidden information, stochastic card draws, combinatorial scoring, multi-agent interaction, and risk-sensitive stop-or-continue decisions into short episodes. The main Qwen and NVIDIA panels show that performance is not explained by parameter scale alone. Supplementary screens further show positive small-model results under restricted settings and model-specific policy-framing effects. These results are descriptive, not universal model rankings.

Why Go-Stop?

Go-Stop is a compact stochastic decision environment. Each action is chosen under hidden cards, uncertain future draws, opponent interaction, and settlement rules that create heavy lower-tail losses. The Go/Stop decision is a risk-sensitive stopping problem: continuing can increase payoff, but it can also amplify losses.

The evaluation therefore uses mean profit together with CVaR 5%, win rate, ruin probability, and sample count. This framing is also methodologically relevant to industrial decision systems such as real-time advertising bidding and campaign budget pacing, where agents repeatedly decide whether to spend, wait, or adjust bids under uncertain returns and downside risk.

Research Questions

  1. RQ1. Why is Go-Stop a plausible environment for evaluating risk-sensitive sequential decision making by LLM agents?
  2. RQ2. In a Qwen-labeled DashScope panel, is observed Go-Stop performance monotone in nominal parameter scale?
  3. RQ3. In a rate-limited NVIDIA Build panel of roughly same-scale models, are cross-family differences visible in descriptive profit and risk summaries?
  4. RQ4. In supplementary screens, do small models and prompt-policy variants show measurable but model-specific differences?

RQ2 is descriptively negative in the Qwen panel. RQ3 shows visible descriptive differences under the constrained NVIDIA panel. RQ4 is exploratory: small-model and policy-framing screens show measurable differences, but not confirmatory universal rankings.

Limitations

The model panels are convenience samples from available remote APIs. The Qwen comparison mixes Coder and Qwen3.5 variants, so parameter scale is partly confounded with specialization. The NVIDIA run measures constrained first-decision behavior more than full-game autonomous play because only one remote call per agent was permitted in each session. Policy screens have 24 samples per model-policy cell and are supplementary.
